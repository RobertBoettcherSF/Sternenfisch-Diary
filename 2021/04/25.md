# 2021-04-25 — Legends, Stories from the Murkelei, The fight with the demon, and The Notes of Malte Laurids Brigge

**Date:** 2021-04-25

**Title:** Legends

**Author:** Stefan Zweig

**Themes:** Legend, Faith, Story

## Synopsis: Legends — Stefan Zweig

Legends collects Stefan Zweig's retellings of legendary material, stories that turn on mercy, recognition, and a moral test. He uses old plots to ask what a person owes a stranger.

The manner is clear and dramatic, closer to a novella than to a sermon.

They suit a reader who wants a short tale with a consequence.

**Date:** 2021-04-25

**Title:** Stories from the Murkelei

**Author:** Hans Fallada

**Themes:** Childhood, Region, Tale

## Synopsis: Stories from the Murkelei — Hans Fallada

Stories from the Murkelei is Hans Fallada's stories from the Murkelei, a country of his own naming, closer to memory and fable than to the Berlin of his big novels. The pieces are lighter, local, and odd.

He still notices how people actually behave. The softness is not emptiness.

It is a book of tales from a made place, meant to be visited one story at a time.

**Date:** 2021-04-25

**Title:** The fight with the demon

**Author:** Stefan Zweig

**Themes:** Genius, Madness, Poetry

## Synopsis: The fight with the demon — Stefan Zweig

The fight with the demon gathers Zweig's essays on Hölderlin, Kleist, and Nietzsche, three writers he saw as driven past the point where a life can stay intact. The 'demon' of the title is the force that makes the work and breaks the person.

Each essay follows the same curve: early promise, isolation, and a mind that will not settle for a manageable career.

It is criticism written as drama, about the price of an uncompromising inner life.

**Date:** 2021-04-25

**Title:** The Notes of Malte Laurids Brigge

**Author:** Rainer Maria Rilke

**Themes:** Paris, Fear, Memory

## Synopsis: The Notes of Malte Laurids Brigge — Rainer Maria Rilke

The Notes of Malte Laurids Brigge is Rilke's notebook of Malte Laurids Brigge, a young Dane alone in Paris. Poverty, hospital faces, and the sudden intensity of childhood memories break the diary into prose poems.

There is no conventional plot to finish. The book asks whether a person can learn, as the famous passage says, to see.

It is a city novel in which the city keeps turning into an inner life.
