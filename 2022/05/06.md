**Title:** Fridolin the cheeky badger

**Author:** Hans Fallada

**Themes:** Children, Animals, Mischief

## Synopsis: Fridolin the cheeky badger — Hans Fallada

Fridolin the cheeky badger is a children's story by Hans Fallada about a cheeky badger. The subtitle on some editions adds a two-legged figure as well as a four-legged one, which is enough to see the joke: animal manners and human ones sharing a tale.

It is not one of his grim novels of money. The promise is mischief, a wood, and a character who will not stay polite.

Adults who know only the Berlin books may be surprised by how light his hand can be.

**Title:** Letters to Ottla and the Family

**Author:** Franz Kafka

**Themes:** Family, Letters, Sister

## Synopsis: Letters to Ottla and the Family — Franz Kafka

Letters to Ottla and the Family collects Kafka's letters to his sister Ottla and to the family around her. They are more intimate and more practical than the fiction: health, apartments, the war, and the relief of writing to someone who took his side.

Ottla is the relative with whom he could be least defended. The letters show affection, advice, and the ordinary logistics of staying close.

Read beside the letter to his father, they are the other temperature of the same household.

**Title:** Marceline Desbordes-Valmore

**Author:** Stefan Zweig

**Themes:** Poetry, Biography, Woman

## Synopsis: Marceline Desbordes-Valmore — Stefan Zweig

Marceline Desbordes-Valmore is Zweig's life of Marceline Desbordes-Valmore, the French poet whose work he helped bring to readers who had overlooked it. A woman's lyric life, hardship, and persistence are the story.

The subtitle on some editions calls it the life of a poetess. The book takes that life seriously as an artistic fact, not as a footnote to famous men.

It belongs with Zweig's recoveries of figures he thought the age had mismeasured.

**Title:** Marie Antoinette

**Author:** Stefan Zweig

**Themes:** Court, Revolution, Portrait

## Synopsis: Marie Antoinette — Stefan Zweig

Marie Antoinette is Stefan Zweig's life of Marie Antoinette, from the Austrian child sent to Versailles to the woman tried by the Revolution. He is interested in the person inside the legend of luxury and indifference.

The court, the pamphlets, the flight to Varennes, and the prison are stages in a character he watches harden and clarify. Sympathy does not cancel the blindness of the old court.

It is a portrait of a queen formed by ceremony and then stripped of it.

**Title:** Sparrow Farm

**Author:** Hans Fallada

**Themes:** Country, Escape, Clerk

## Synopsis: Sparrow Farm — Hans Fallada

Sparrow Farm announces itself in the subtitle as the tale of a town clerk who fled to the countryside. Fallada's interest in that flight is practical: what a small holding demands, and what a city person imagines it will cure.

The note will not add scenes beyond that framing. A clerk, a rural experiment, and the hope of a different life are the story the title offers.

It sits with his other books about ordinary people trying to change the terms of their work.

**Title:** Three poets, of their life

**Author:** Stefan Zweig

**Themes:** Biography, Adventure, Writers

## Synopsis: Three poets, of their life — Stefan Zweig

Three poets, of their life is Zweig's triptych of Casanova, Stendhal, and Tolstoy, three men who tried to turn life itself into a work. Seduction, ambition, and a late religious crisis are the three answers he studies.

He is curious about how a writer uses his own days as material, and about the moment when that use fails.

The book belongs with his other portrait-essays: short, vivid, and more interested in character than in a full archive.

**Title:** Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus

**Author:** Ludwig Josef Johann Wittgenstein

**Themes:** Language, Logic, Limits

## Synopsis: Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus — Ludwig Josef Johann Wittgenstein

Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus is Wittgenstein's austere book on what can be said clearly and what must be shown. Numbered propositions climb from a picture theory of sentences toward ethics, the mystical, and the famous closing demand for silence.

The ladder, he says, is to be thrown away after one has climbed it. The book both builds a logical world and marks its border.

It is difficult, brief, and unlike any other work of philosophy in its form.
